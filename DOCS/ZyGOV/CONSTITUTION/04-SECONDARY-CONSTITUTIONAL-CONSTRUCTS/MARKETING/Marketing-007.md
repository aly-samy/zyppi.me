# 0. Purpose

Zyppi is ambitious enough to generate attractive internal theories faster than the market can validate them.

That creates danger.

A feature can look differentiated until a competitor already offers it.

A pricing model can look elegant until the implied revenue is too small to support the company.

A buyer can look obvious until the person using the product has no authority or budget.

A workflow can look valuable until the incumbent vertical application already owns the job.

An enterprise opportunity can look lucrative until implementation consumes more scarce human resources than the revenue justifies.

A low-cost utility can look strategically useful until Feature Gravity turns it into an unrelated SaaS company.

The purpose of `Marketing-007` is to establish a repeatable constitutional protocol for attacking such hypotheses before Zyppi commits disproportionate resources.

The attack protocol SHALL help Zyppi determine:

- what the real customer problem is;
- what the buyer does today;
- which alternatives already solve the job;
- which capabilities are commoditizing;
- whether standards or open source erase feature-level differentiation;
- whether a competent customer can build the solution internally;
- whether willingness to pay is evidenced;
- whether distribution is plausible;
- whether the human-resource burden is survivable;
- whether COGS scale;
- whether the product compounds reusable Zyppi value;
- whether the hypothesis should survive, change role, or die.

The central rule is:

> **Attack the hypothesis hard enough to discover its strongest surviving form—not merely hard enough to destroy it.**

# 1. Authority Boundary

`Marketing-007` does not define what evidence means.

That authority belongs to `Marketing-001`.

The relationship is:

```text
Marketing-001
What counts as evidence?
How strong is the claim?

        ↓

Marketing-007
How do we systematically attack the claim?

        ↓

Marketing-011
How do we run real experiments to create E3 / E4 evidence?
```

`Marketing-007` SHALL use the E0–E4 evidence ladder without redefining it.

---

# 2. Market Attack Definition

A **Market Attack** is a structured strategic stress test applied to a material marketing, product, pricing, entry, buyer, distribution, or economic hypothesis.

Its purpose is to discover:

1. what survives;
2. what fails;
3. what belongs in a different strategic role;
4. what evidence is still missing;
5. what should be tested next.

An attack is not a rhetorical debate.

It is an evidence-producing decision process.

---

# 3. Attack Is Not Falsification Theater

The Marketing branch rejects the idea that intelligence is demonstrated by destroying every proposal.

An attack SHALL NOT begin with:

> "How do we prove this is wrong?"

It SHOULD begin with:

> **"What would have to be true for this to work, what could invalidate it, and what is the strongest version that survives?"**

A strong attack may conclude:

- PASS;
- PROVISIONAL PASS;
- PRESERVE;
- DEMOTE;
- REWORK;
- REJECT;
- HOLD.

Rejection is one valid outcome.

It is not the objective.

---

# 4. Constitutional Law — Attack Before Commitment

Material strategic hypotheses SHOULD be attacked before Zyppi commits:

- major engineering effort;
- major marketing spend;
- irreversible product architecture;
- long-term pricing structure;
- significant human support;
- major partnerships;
- constitutional doctrine.

The intensity of the attack SHOULD be proportional to:

```text
cost
+
irreversibility
+
consequence
+
strategic importance
```

A tiny reversible test does not need an exhaustive Council inquiry.

A multi-year platform commitment does.

---

# 5. Constitutional Law — Competition Includes All Alternatives

Competitive intelligence SHALL include more than direct vendors.

The relevant competitive set may include:

- direct competitors;
- adjacent products;
- vertical applications;
- horizontal platforms;
- standards;
- open-source projects;
- cloud primitives;
- customer internal build;
- manual process;
- spreadsheets;
- consultants;
- system integrators;
- existing incumbent stack;
- doing nothing.

The correct question is:

> **What would the buyer actually do if Zyppi did not exist?**

---

# 6. Constitutional Law — Whole Systems Before Feature Unions

The attack protocol inherits `Marketing-003`.

Zyppi SHALL compare:

```text
one coherent Zyppi system
```

against:

```text
one coherent alternative
```

or the realistic combination of systems the buyer already operates.

A union of every competitor's best individual features may be used as an extreme stress test.

It SHALL NOT be mistaken for one actual competitor.

---

# 7. Constitutional Law — Market Attack Must Preserve Evidence Grade

An attack can strengthen reasoning.

It cannot manufacture E3.

For example:

```text
competitor comparison
+
economic modeling
+
AI buyer simulation
```

may strengthen E1/E2+ reasoning.

It does not prove that a real buyer will pay Zyppi.

The final Attack Receipt SHALL preserve this distinction.

---

# 8. Constitutional Law — Negative Evidence Must Land

If the attack discovers that:

- competitors already provide the capability free;
- the buyer has no budget;
- the job is already owned by another system;
- human implementation is uneconomic;
- infrastructure cost destroys margins;
- standards commoditize the function;

the finding SHALL affect the strategic conclusion.

Research SHALL NOT become decoration around a pre-decided answer.

---

# 9. Constitutional Law — Preserved Value Must Also Land

A failed primary thesis may still contain a useful capability.

Example:

```text
Hypothesis:
Signals are the first paid product.

Attack:
Signals alone are too sparse.

Outcome:
PRESERVE signals inside Interaction Intelligence.
```

The protocol SHALL preserve reusable value rather than discard it merely because its original role failed.

# 10. Attack Scope Classes

The Marketing branch recognizes four attack scopes.

---

## 10.1 Atomic Capability Attack

Question:

> Is this individual capability useful, differentiated, and economically meaningful?

Examples:

- routing;
- signal;
- webhook replay;
- verification;
- state endpoint.

---

## 10.2 Product-System Attack

Question:

> Does the coherent bundle create a better complete outcome than alternatives?

Examples:

- Interaction Intelligence;
- Resolver + contextual policy + history;
- Trust + execution workflow.

---

## 10.3 Market-Entry Attack

Question:

> Is this the right doorway for Zyppi to establish a market?

Evaluates:

- adoption friction;
- distribution;
- incumbent attention;
- durable footprint;
- compounding.

---

## 10.4 Economic-System Attack

Question:

> Can this commercial model support both customer fairness and Zyppi sustainability?

Examples:

- fixed SaaS tier;
- ECU consumption;
- free standards subsidy;
- bootstrap utility model.

---

# 11. Attack Depth

An attack may be:

## Rapid

Used for small reversible hypotheses.

---

## Standard

Used for product and pricing decisions.

---

## Deep

Used for:

- major market entry;
- constitutional doctrine;
- significant engineering commitments;
- seed-funding thesis;
- domain expansion.

Deep attacks SHOULD seek E2+ triangulation before action where practical.

# 12. Attack Phase A — Define the Hypothesis

Every attack SHALL begin with one falsifiable proposition.

Weak:

> "Webhook tools are interesting."

Better:

> "A self-service webhook inbox can generate low-support bootstrap revenue while hardening event infrastructure Zyppi needs later."

The hypothesis SHOULD identify:

- target actor;
- job;
- proposed value;
- market role;
- economic role;
- expected strategic carryover.

---

# 13. Define the Proposed Role

A capability can succeed in one role and fail in another.

Possible roles include:

- Trojan Horse;
- free acquisition layer;
- activation layer;
- paid product;
- revenue engine;
- supporting capability;
- infrastructure primitive;
- enterprise feature;
- bootstrap utility;
- distribution surface.

The attack SHALL specify the proposed role.

Otherwise a useful capability may be rejected merely because it failed the wrong job.

---

# 14. Attack Phase B — Customer Need

Ask:

1. What problem exists?
2. How often does it occur?
3. How painful is it?
4. Who experiences it?
5. What happens if unresolved?
6. Is it urgent?
7. Is urgency structural or temporary?
8. Is the problem large enough to change behavior?

Evidence SHOULD distinguish:

```text
important in theory
```

from:

```text
urgent enough to act
```

---

# 15. Pain Severity

The attack SHOULD evaluate pain across:

- cost;
- time;
- risk;
- compliance;
- revenue;
- customer experience;
- operational complexity;
- manual effort.

A mild annoyance may support a free utility.

It may not support a high-value paid workflow.

---

# 16. Frequency vs Severity

A problem may be:

- frequent but low consequence;
- rare but high consequence.

Both can support products.

The commercial model may differ.

Frequency SHALL not be mistaken for value.

Severity SHALL not be mistaken for market size.

---

# 17. Attack Phase C — Current Alternative

The attack SHALL document what the target actor does today.

Possible current alternatives:

- existing vendor;
- internal tool;
- spreadsheet;
- static link;
- manual process;
- agency;
- no action.

This creates the real baseline.

Zyppi must beat the current behavior—not an imagined empty market.

---

# 18. Status Quo Cost

The attack SHOULD estimate:

- money;
- effort;
- failure risk;
- switching resistance;
- organizational inertia.

If the status quo is "good enough," Zyppi requires a stronger trigger.

---

# 19. Attack Phase D — Direct Competition

For relevant competitors, collect where practical:

- exact capability;
- price;
- free tier;
- limits;
- integrations;
- positioning;
- buyer;
- distribution;
- switching model;
- source date.

Competitor research SHOULD use primary sources where possible.

---

# 20. Competitor Truth vs Competitor Marketing

The attack SHALL distinguish:

```text
vendor claims capability
```

from:

```text
independent evidence capability works well
```

Official sources may prove existence and price.

They do not always prove quality or adoption.

---

# 21. Attack Phase E — Adjacent Competition

Many Zyppi hypotheses fail because an adjacent product already owns the buyer's workflow.

Examples may include:

- warranty SaaS;
- PIM;
- ERP;
- QR platform;
- feature-flag system;
- workflow engine.

The attack SHALL ask:

> **Who already owns the screen, data, workflow, or budget?**

---

# 22. Workflow Ownership Test

A competitor may not replicate Zyppi's architecture.

It may still control the job because it owns:

- system of record;
- daily operator workflow;
- customer relationship;
- budget;
- integration.

This can be more strategically important than feature similarity.

---

# 23. Attack Phase F — Standards Pressure

The attack SHOULD determine whether:

- open standards already solve the primitive;
- the standard is expanding;
- standards bodies are commoditizing the layer;
- compliance requires the standard but not Zyppi.

If a standard moves downward into the capability, Zyppi should usually move upward in value.

---

# 24. Standards Sunrise vs Vendor Mandate

The attack SHALL preserve:

```text
standard adoption deadline
≠
requirement to purchase Zyppi
```

Regulatory or standards momentum may create urgency.

It does not select Zyppi automatically.

---

# 25. Attack Phase G — Open Source Pressure

Ask:

1. Is there a mature open-source substitute?
2. How difficult is deployment?
3. How difficult is operation?
4. What managed value remains?

A mature open-source implementation may reduce pricing power at the feature layer.

It may increase demand for managed infrastructure.

---

# 26. Attack Phase H — Cloud Primitive Pressure

Cloud services may commoditize:

- routing;
- state;
- logging;
- storage;
- functions;
- messaging;
- monitoring;
- AI.

The attack SHOULD compare Zyppi against the primitive stack a competent developer could assemble.

---

# 27. Build-It-Yourself Test

Ask:

> **Could the target customer build the required production outcome internally at acceptable cost and risk?**

Evaluate full burden:

- development;
- standards maintenance;
- uptime;
- security;
- observability;
- data model;
- retries;
- state;
- versioning;
- support;
- governance.

A 20-line prototype is not a production comparison.

---

# 28. Attack Phase I — Commoditization Test

A capability is under strong commoditization pressure when several of the following are true:

- multiple vendors include it free;
- open standards provide it;
- cloud primitives make it trivial;
- open source is mature;
- switching is easy;
- buyer treats it as expected.

Possible attack outcome:

```text
PRESERVE as doorway
DEMOTE as differentiator
```

rather than automatic rejection.

---

# 29. Commodity Doorway Test

If a capability is commodity, ask:

> **Can it still acquire users cheaply and leave behind durable Zyppi assets?**

If yes, it may remain a strong Trojan Horse under `Marketing-002`.

---

# 30. Attack Phase J — Product-System Alternative

The attack SHALL evaluate the complete buyer outcome.

Ask:

- How many systems are required?
- What integrations are needed?
- Where does context live?
- Who explains decisions?
- What must be reconciled?
- What history is fragmented?

This tests Integration Compression.

---

# 31. Integration Compression Test

Estimate the difference between:

```text
alternative stack
```

and:

```text
Zyppi system
```

in:

- number of integrations;
- operational systems;
- handoffs;
- data translations;
- policy locations;
- audit sources.

Integration compression can support system differentiation.

---

# 32. Attack Phase K — Buyer Identification

The attack SHALL identify:

- user;
- technical buyer;
- economic buyer;
- approver;
- operator;
- blocker;
- champion.

One person may occupy several roles.

The attack SHALL not assume they are the same.

---

# 33. Buyer Authority Test

Ask:

> **Does the person who experiences the pain control the budget or decision?**

If no, define the Buyer Bridge required to move from user value to purchase.

This inherits `Marketing-004`.

---

# 34. Buyer Bridge Test

A proposed expansion path SHALL identify how value travels between buying centers.

Example:

```text
Developer
→ operational proof
→ manager
→ risk team
→ governance buyer
```

Without a credible bridge, vertical capability progression may not become commercial progression.

---

# 35. Attack Phase L — Jobs-To-Be-Done

The attack SHOULD express the proposition in buyer job language.

Format:

> **When [situation], I need to [job], so I can [outcome].**

The product architecture may be complex.

The job should be comprehensible.

---

# 36. Buyer Language Test

Ask:

> **Can the buyer describe the problem without using Zyppi terminology?**

If not, the proposition may be architecture-first rather than job-first.

---

# 37. Attack Phase M — Why Now?

A viable entry often needs a trigger.

Possible triggers:

- regulation;
- standards transition;
- channel requirement;
- operational failure;
- cost pressure;
- new AI workflow;
- growth;
- audit;
- customer demand.

The attack SHOULD identify the trigger.

---

# 38. Urgency Test

Ask:

> **What happens if the buyer waits 12 months?**

If the answer is:

> "almost nothing"

the proposition may face strong status quo inertia.

---

# 39. Attack Phase N — Willingness to Pay

The attack SHALL distinguish:

```text
value exists
```

from:

```text
buyer will pay Zyppi
```

Evidence may include:

- competitor prices;
- adjacent budgets;
- direct customer payment;
- price tests.

Only real Zyppi behavior produces E3/E4 willingness-to-pay evidence.

---

# 40. Price Anchor Test

External prices can establish:

- plausible market bands;
- existing spend categories.

They SHALL NOT determine Zyppi's final price automatically.

The attack SHOULD compare:

```text
Zyppi expected monthly bill
```

against:

```text
coherent alternatives
+
status quo cost
+
customer value
```

---

# 41. Attack Phase O — Revenue Density

The attack SHOULD estimate how much revenue can realistically be generated from the expected usage pattern.

A model may fail even if the unit price appears reasonable.

Example:

```text
$1 / 1,000 operations
×
too little early traffic
=
insufficient startup revenue
```

This is a valid strategic finding.

---

# 42. Revenue Density vs Market Size

A huge market with tiny revenue per participant may be a weak survival engine.

A small market with large human service burden may also be weak.

The attack SHALL consider:

```text
Revenue
per
unit of scarce Zyppi effort
```

---

# 43. Attack Phase P — Human Resource Burden

Evaluate expected:

- sales effort;
- onboarding;
- configuration;
- support;
- custom engineering;
- account management;
- compliance work.

During Baby:

> **Human attention is often scarcer than compute.**

---

# 44. Human Efficiency Ratio

Plans MAY model:

```text
Recurring Gross Contribution
÷
Human Hours Required
```

to compare opportunities.

The exact ratio is not constitutionalized.

The principle is:

> **revenue quality depends partly upon how much human attention it consumes.**

---

# 45. Design-Partner Warning

A design-partner motion may produce learning and revenue.

It is dangerous when:

- acquisition is slow;
- every customer requires custom work;
- pricing does not cover effort;
- reuse is low.

The attack SHOULD classify design-partner economics separately from scalable product economics.

---

# 46. Attack Phase Q — COGS

Estimate:

- compute;
- requests;
- storage;
- event writes;
- retention;
- messaging;
- AI;
- payment processing;
- monitoring.

Cost estimates SHALL preserve source date.

---

# 47. Direct COGS vs Total Cost-to-Serve

The attack SHALL distinguish:

```text
cloud cost
```

from:

```text
total cost-to-serve
```

Total cost may include:

- support;
- abuse;
- observability;
- compliance;
- payment fees.

Cheap compute does not guarantee cheap business operations.

---

# 48. Attack Phase R — Margin

Ask:

- Is the model structurally capable of sustainable margin?
- Does margin improve with scale?
- Is high human support hiding inside COGS?

The attack SHALL not approve an apparently profitable utility if support burden makes it uneconomic.

---

# 49. Attack Phase S — Distribution

The attack SHALL identify how the target user discovers Zyppi.

Possible channels:

- search;
- developer ecosystem;
- package managers;
- marketplaces;
- integrations;
- partners;
- physical carriers;
- standards communities;
- outbound sales.

"No distribution plan" is a material weakness.

---

# 50. Distribution Cost Test

Ask:

> **Can acquisition cost plausibly fit the expected lifetime economic value?**

Exact CAC may be unknown at E1/E2.

The structural fit should still be assessed.

---

# 51. Embedded Distribution Test

A capability is stronger when use itself can create distribution.

Examples:

- public reference;
- API integration;
- partner embed;
- plugin;
- physical label.

This may strengthen a Trojan Horse even when direct monetization is weak.

---

# 52. Attack Phase T — Adoption Friction

Evaluate:

- signup;
- implementation;
- data preparation;
- content preparation;
- API integration;
- procurement;
- training;
- migration.

A better product can lose because adoption is harder.

---

# 53. Time-to-Value Test

Ask:

> **How long until the user experiences the first real outcome?**

Entry products SHOULD generally optimize for minutes or hours where possible.

High-consequence workflows may justify longer activation.

---

# 54. Attack Phase U — Cognitive Load

Evaluate whether the buyer must understand:

- new category;
- complex architecture;
- unfamiliar terminology;
- too many pricing units.

High cognitive load increases status quo bias.

---

# 55. Positioning Firewall Test

At entry:

> **Can Zyppi explain the immediate buyer job without leading with the universal platform?**

If not, positioning may be too abstract.

This inherits `Marketing-003`.

---

# 56. Attack Phase V — Strategic Carryover

Ask:

> **What reusable Zyppi capability remains if this product succeeds?**

Possible outputs:

- resolver;
- event ingestion;
- state;
- policy;
- monitoring;
- Evidence;
- authority;
- receipt;
- developer distribution.

---

# 57. Reuse Yield

A product with moderate revenue but high reusable capability yield may be strategically superior to a higher-revenue unrelated application.

Plans SHOULD assess:

```text
cash yield
+
platform reuse yield
```

---

# 58. Attack Phase W — Feature Gravity

Ask:

> **What adjacent requests will this product naturally attract?**

Then ask:

> **Would satisfying them turn Zyppi into another category?**

A useful utility can be rejected if the likely roadmap creates unacceptable Feature Gravity.

---

# 59. Feature Gravity Escalation Pattern

Examples:

```text
simple menu
→ page builder
→ ordering
→ POS
```

or:

```text
short link
→ campaigns
→ CRM
→ marketing automation
```

The attack SHOULD model likely pull, not merely MVP scope.

---

# 60. Attack Phase X — North Star Carryover

A candidate need not expose the North Star directly.

It SHOULD avoid blocking it.

Ask:

- Does the architecture remain reusable?
- Does it create incompatible semantics?
- Does it consume resources needed for core platform?
- Does it strengthen or weaken future optionality?

---

# 61. Strategic Alignment Is Not Sufficient

A product may align beautifully with the North Star and still be a bad market entry.

Market reality remains required.

---

# 62. Attack Phase Y — Compounding

Evaluate:

## Horizontal

Does adoption bring more participants?

## Vertical

Does use enable deeper Zyppi?

## Temporal

Does history become more valuable?

A strong candidate should ideally compound in at least one meaningful dimension.

---

# 63. Compounding Quality

The attack SHALL distinguish:

```text
more data
```

from:

```text
more valuable context
```

and:

```text
more users
```

from:

```text
more value for other users
```

---

# 64. Attack Phase Z — Moat Relevance

The attack SHOULD classify the candidate's long-term defensibility.

Possible states:

- commodity;
- entry differentiation;
- product-system differentiation;
- compounding advantage;
- moat hypothesis.

A Baby product does not need a moat.

It needs a credible role.

# 66. Attack Scoring

Programs MAY use numeric scores.

The Constitution SHALL NOT require a single weighted formula.

A mechanical score can hide a fatal weakness.

Example:

A candidate may score well overall but fail because:

- no buyer exists;
- human effort is impossible;
- regulation prohibits it.

Fatal gates outrank averages.

---

# 67. Fatal Gates

A material candidate SHOULD be rejected or deeply reworked if any of the following is true and cannot be resolved:

- no meaningful problem;
- no accessible user/buyer;
- no distribution;
- impossible human burden;
- structurally uneconomic COGS;
- constitutional conflict;
- unacceptable Feature Gravity;
- no reusable value and no sufficient standalone economics.

---

# 68. Strategic Trade-Offs

Most candidates will contain mixed evidence.

Example:

```text
high adoption
+
low revenue
+
high infrastructure reuse
```

This may be a strong Trojan Horse.

Another:

```text
high revenue
+
high human customization
+
low reuse
```

may be a poor Baby strategy.

The attack SHALL evaluate role-fit rather than one universal score.

---

# 69. Attack Outcome Vocabulary

Every material attack SHALL end with one explicit outcome.

---

## PASS

The hypothesis survives in its proposed role with sufficient support for the current decision stage.

PASS does not imply E4 validation.

---

## PROVISIONAL PASS

The hypothesis survives but contains material uncertainty requiring experiment or evidence.

---

## PRESERVE

The capability remains strategically useful, but not in the proposed primary role.

---

## DEMOTE

The capability should move to a lower economic, product, or strategic layer.

---

## REWORK

The core insight survives but requires material redesign.

---

## REJECT

The hypothesis should not carry further strategic reliance under current evidence.

---

## HOLD

Evidence is insufficient, contradictory, or premature.

---

# 70. Outcome Is Role-Specific

Example:

```text
Static contextual routing
```

may be:

```text
REJECT
as first paid wedge

PRESERVE
as free entry capability
```

This is not contradiction.

It is role-specific judgment.

# 74. Desk Research Role

Desk research is strongest for:

# 99A. Operational Timebox Principle

Every Standard, P1, or P2 attack SHALL receive an explicit timebox in the current program or decision log.

The Constitution SHALL NOT impose one universal number of hours or days because research difficulty varies by question. However:

- P2 attacks SHALL NOT delay authorized market contact;
- reversible P1 decisions SHOULD move rapidly to experiment once additional E2 research is unlikely to change the decision;
- when the timebox expires with material uncertainty, the default outcomes are **PROVISIONAL PASS**, **HOLD**, or escalation to an E3 experiment—not indefinite desk research.

P0 attacks may require deeper investigation when the unresolved issue could make execution unsafe, illegal, structurally uneconomic, or strategically fatal.

The governing rule is:

> **Every attack needs a clock. When research stops changing the decision, Reality gets the next turn.**

## Anti-Pattern 3 — Competitor Union

Treating the union of all competitor features as one coherent alternative.

---

## Anti-Pattern 4 — Competitor Dismissal

Ignoring a specialist because Zyppi's long-term vision is larger.

---

## Anti-Pattern 5 — Architecture Defense

Responding to market weakness by explaining more architecture.

---

## Anti-Pattern 6 — WTP by ROI Spreadsheet

Assuming calculated value proves willingness to pay.

---

## Anti-Pattern 7 — Cloud-Cost Pricing

Assuming low infrastructure cost determines customer price.

---

## Anti-Pattern 8 — Human-Cost Blindness

Ignoring onboarding and support.

---

## Anti-Pattern 9 — One-Failure Total Rejection

Discarding all useful capability because one commercial role fails.

---

## Anti-Pattern 10 — Role Ambiguity

Attacking a capability without defining whether it is a doorway, product, or revenue engine.

---

## Anti-Pattern 11 — Endless Research

Refusing to test because more analysis is possible.

---

## Anti-Pattern 12 — Simulated Validation

Treating AI or Council consensus as E3 evidence.

# 110. Closing Principle

Zyppi will repeatedly encounter ideas that look compelling on paper.

Some will survive.

Some will fail.

Some will be valuable for an entirely different reason than first imagined.

The purpose of market intelligence is not to protect the original idea.

It is to protect Zyppi from investing in the wrong version of the idea.

The Marketing branch therefore adopts:

> **Define the hypothesis. Find the real alternative. Attack the buyer, urgency, competition, economics, human burden, distribution, compounding, and constitutional carryover. Preserve what survives. Kill what does not. Then stop researching when only Reality can answer.**

And:

> **A failed hypothesis is cheap. An unchallenged assumption embedded in the company is expensive.**
